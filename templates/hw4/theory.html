Theory: edge detection and segmentation in the frequency domain

Edges are, by definition, places where intensity changes quickly over a short spatial distance - i.e. they are dominated by high spatial frequencies. Regions are the opposite: areas of slowly-varying intensity, i.e. low spatial frequencies. That single observation is what lets both edge detection and region segmentation be derived as frequency-domain filtering operations.

1. Differentiation theorem → gradient edge detectors are high-pass filters

The Fourier transform of a partial derivative is multiplication by frequency:

$$ \mathcal{F}\left\{ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \right\}(u,v) = j2\pi u \, F(u,v), \qquad \mathcal{F}\left\{ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \right\}(u,v) = j2\pi v \, F(u,v) $$

This is proved directly from the transform's definition via integration by parts, using $f \to 0$ at infinity to drop the boundary term:

$$ \mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right\} = \int\!\!\int \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} e^{-j2\pi(ux+vy)}\,dx\,dy = \Big[f\,e^{-j2\pi(ux+vy)}\Big]_{x=-\infty}^{\infty} + j2\pi u \int\!\!\int f\, e^{-j2\pi(ux+vy)}\,dx\,dy = j2\pi u\, F(u,v) $$

So a spatial derivative operator (Sobel, Prewitt) corresponds in the frequency domain to multiplying by $j2\pi u$ (or $j2\pi v$) - a filter whose gain grows linearly with frequency, i.e. a high-pass filter. That's why gradient operators respond strongly at edges (high frequency) and are near-zero in flat regions (low frequency, near $u=v=0$).

2. Laplacian → an isotropic (radial) high-pass filter

Applying the derivative theorem twice, once per axis, and summing:

$$ \mathcal{F}\{\nabla^2 f\}(u,v) = \mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}\right\} = (j2\pi u)^2 F + (j2\pi v)^2 F = -4\pi^2(u^2+v^2)\, F(u,v) $$

Writing $\rho = \sqrt{u^2+v^2}$ for radial frequency, the Laplacian's frequency response is $H(u,v) = -4\pi^2 \rho^2$ - a high-pass filter that depends only on distance from the origin (isotropic, unlike the directional $j2\pi u$ response above). This is exactly why Laplacian-based edge detection (zero-crossings of $\nabla^2 f$) is equivalent to applying a radial high-pass filter in the frequency domain and finding where the filtered signal changes sign.

3. General frequency-domain edge enhancement

Any edge detector of this family can be written as filtering in the frequency domain and inverting:

$$ g(x,y) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\, H_{hp}(u,v)\, F(u,v) \,\} $$

with $H_{hp}$ an ideal, Butterworth, or Gaussian high-pass filter (the smooth Butterworth/Gaussian variants avoid the ringing that an ideal brick-wall cutoff introduces via the Gibbs phenomenon). By the convolution theorem (see Homework 3 theory), this is identical to convolving $f$ with $h_{hp} = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{H_{hp}\}$ in the spatial domain - which is precisely what a Sobel or Laplacian kernel is: a small, spatially-compact approximation to an ideal high-pass filter.

4. Region segmentation as low-pass / band-limited filtering

Segmentation asks the opposite question: which pixels belong to the same slowly-varying region? Three frequency-domain views used in practice:

  • Low-pass smoothing before thresholding. $g = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{H_{lp}(u,v) F(u,v)\}$ suppresses high-frequency noise/texture while preserving the coarse region structure, making a global threshold (e.g. Otsu) more reliable. This is exactly the Gaussian blur pre-step used before Otsu thresholding in the thermal segmentation on this page - a literal application of Homework 3's low-pass filter, used here to stabilize a segmentation threshold rather than for aesthetics.
  • Homomorphic filtering. Writing $f(x,y) = i(x,y)\, r(x,y)$ (illumination times reflectance) and taking logs turns the product into a sum, $\ln f = \ln i + \ln r$, which is now linear and can be Fourier-filtered: illumination varies slowly (low frequency), reflectance/texture varies quickly (high frequency). A high-pass filter in this log-Fourier domain suppresses uneven illumination while keeping object detail, then $\exp(\cdot)$ inverts the log - improving threshold-based segmentation under non-uniform lighting.
  • Band-pass / local spectral texture features. Computing the local power spectrum in sliding windows (or with Gabor filters, which are localized band-pass filters in both space and frequency) characterizes texture per region; pixels/windows with similar local spectra are grouped as the same region. This generalizes simple intensity-threshold segmentation to texture-based segmentation.

Tying it back to the two implementations on this page

Both classical segmenters on this site are, in this framework, spatial-domain shortcuts for frequency-domain ideas: GrabCut's boundary term implicitly favors compact, smooth regions (low-frequency structure) over scattered foreground pixels, and the thermal pipeline's Gaussian pre-blur is literally a low-pass filter (Homework 3) applied to stabilize the Otsu threshold against sensor noise before the high-frequency region boundary is extracted as a contour.
