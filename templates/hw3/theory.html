Theory: the convolution theorem

Claim: convolving an image with a filter kernel in the spatial domain gives the same result as multiplying their Fourier transforms and taking the inverse Fourier transform.

$$ f(x,y) * h(x,y) \;\; \Longleftrightarrow \;\; F(u,v)\cdot H(u,v) $$

Continuous derivation

2D convolution is defined as

$$ (f*h)(x,y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(\tau_1,\tau_2)\, h(x-\tau_1,\, y-\tau_2)\, d\tau_1\, d\tau_2 $$

Take the 2D Fourier transform of both sides (transforming over $x,y$):

$$ \mathcal{F}\{f*h\}(u,v) = \int\!\!\int \left[ \int\!\!\int f(\tau_1,\tau_2)\, h(x-\tau_1, y-\tau_2)\, d\tau_1 d\tau_2 \right] e^{-j2\pi(ux+vy)}\, dx\, dy $$

Swap the order of integration and substitute $x' = x-\tau_1,\; y' = y-\tau_2$:

$$ = \int\!\!\int f(\tau_1,\tau_2)\, e^{-j2\pi(u\tau_1+v\tau_2)} d\tau_1 d\tau_2 \;\cdot\; \int\!\!\int h(x',y')\, e^{-j2\pi(ux'+vy')} dx'\, dy' $$

The two integrals are exactly the definitions of $F(u,v)$ and $H(u,v)$, so

$$ \mathcal{F}\{f*h\}(u,v) = F(u,v)\, H(u,v) $$

The separation into a product works because the complex exponential factors as $e^{-j2\pi(ux+vy)} = e^{-j2\pi(u\tau_1+v\tau_2)}\, e^{-j2\pi(ux'+vy')}$ once the substitution removes the coupling between $\tau$ and $x$ introduced by the shift in $h$.

Discrete case: why the images had to be zero-padded

The DFT version of the theorem is the same statement, but for the discrete circular convolution:

$$ (f \circledast h)[m,n] \;\; \Longleftrightarrow \;\; F[k,l] \cdot H[k,l] $$

A direct FFT multiply on an $M \times N$ image therefore gives circular convolution - the kernel wraps around the image border, which is not what cv2.filter2D computes (it zero-pads at the border, i.e. linear convolution). The standard fix, used in blur.py, is to zero-pad the image by the kernel's radius before the FFT: that gives the wraparound artifacts room to land entirely inside the padding, so the center region of the circular result equals the true linear convolution. This is the classic "linear convolution via padded circular convolution" trick, and it's what makes the spatial and frequency results match to floating-point precision on the blur page rather than just approximately.

Why blurring is a low-pass filter

A blur kernel (box or Gaussian) is a smoothing/averaging operator - it removes fast intensity transitions, which is exactly what high spatial frequencies encode. In the frequency domain this is literal: $H(u,v)$ for a Gaussian kernel is itself a Gaussian, peaked at the origin (DC) and decaying with radius $\rho = \sqrt{u^2+v^2}$. Multiplying $F(u,v)$ by $H(u,v)$ attenuates high-frequency content proportionally to $H$'s falloff, leaving the low frequencies (coarse shading) largely intact - visible directly in the blurred image's shrunken magnitude spectrum on the blur page.
